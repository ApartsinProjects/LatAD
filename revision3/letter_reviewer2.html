Comment 1. Expand each abbreviation at first use (LatAD, IIoT, CPS, SCADA, and so on).

When an abbreviation first appears in the abstract or main text, its full form should be provided, e.g., LatAD, IIoT, CPS, SCADA, etc.

LatAD is given its full form at first use in the abstract ("We present LatAD (Latent-density Anomaly Detector)"), and CPS, IIoT, and SCADA are expanded in Section 1 ("A cyber-physical system (CPS)"; "Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT)"; "Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) historians"). The remaining abbreviations (VaDE, GMM, NLL, HC, BIC, AUROC, F1, ICS, SOTA, KDE) are expanded at first use in the main text; the abstract keeps the compact forms and the main text re-expands them.

Comment 2. The abstract spends most of its length on the problems of existing methods; highlight LatAD's technical contribution.

The abstract spends most of its length introducing the problems of existing anomaly detection methods, while providing very little introduction to the principle of the paper's core contribution, LatAD. Please highlight the technical contributions of this paper in the abstract.

Response: change made. Good point. The abstract states LatAD's principle directly: "Our detector, LatAD (Latent-density Anomaly Detector), learns a latent Gaussian-mixture clustering of regimes (VaDE), scores anomalies by density, and factorizes that density over correlation-community subsystems combined by a cohesion-weighted Higher Criticism statistic." It then reports the headline result: LatAD attains the best All-subset AUROC of any learned detector on HAI (0.948) and a significant lead on the difficult subset (HAI 0.845; the deep detectors USAD and TranAD fall to 0.44 to 0.50 on these reconstructable-but-improbable faults).

The logic of the Introduction is not clear enough and should be carefully revised. In the first paragraph, the last sentence, "This paper detects them by XXX," should be deleted. The contributions are stated later in Section 1, after the problem and the two obstacles are set out.

Response: change made. The sentence now names the task explicitly (Section 1): "Unsupervised anomaly detection for CPS telemetry is often framed as characterizing the anomalies, but that framing is misleading."

Comment 5. The "two obstacles": does LatAD contribute to both, and what problem does it address or improve?

The paper states that modeling and evaluation are "two obstacles that have obscured this failure mode." Has LatAD made contributions to both aspects? What specific problem is LatAD proposed to address, or what existing method does it improve? Please explain clearly.

Response: change made. LatAD contributes to both obstacles, and Section 1 now states this through its three contributions. On the modeling obstacle, contribution C1 replaces the reconstruction residual of USAD- and TranAD-style deep detectors with a probability score in a jointly learned latent regime space, factorized over the plant's physical subsystems. On the evaluation obstacle, contribution C2 is a detector-agnostic difficulty stratification, the channel-based rule and the linear principal-component subspace filter that isolate the difficult and double-hard subsets; the underlying raw-metric protocol it builds on, point-wise scoring with no point adjustment, is adopted from the critique rather than claimed as novel. Contribution C3 adds a drift-aware anomaly typing, so LatAD contributes on both the modeling and the evaluation axes.

Comment 7. Add a system-model or application-scenario diagram showing where in the IIoT LatAD collects data and detects.

Add a system model diagram or an application scenario diagram that shows at which stage of the Industrial IoT the proposed LatAD method collects data and performs anomaly detection.

Response: change made. Good suggestion. Figure 1 (Section 1) shows the IIoT monitoring context: field sensors and PLCs feed an edge gateway or SCADA historian that aggregates and windows the multivariate telemetry, LatAD scores each completed window online, and an anomaly alarm together with the most-surprised subsystem is forwarded to the operator, maintenance, or security stack. It is referenced from Section 1, and the caption notes that model fitting and calibration use train-normal telemetry only, so a site is commissioned from its own normal data without labeled attacks. The most-surprised community Figure 1 forwards is now an evaluated triage output: Section 8 (new Table 5) reports that a three-community shortlist ranked by train-normal p-value contains the attacked subsystem in 68 percent of HAI and 56 percent of WADI attacks, against 56 and 31 percent for a random shortlist of the same size.

Comment 10. Is VaDE plus density plus residual fitted separately per community, or is the encoder shared? Report training time, GPU memory, and inference latency.

Is VaDE + density head + residual head fitted separately for each community? Or is the encoder shared? The paper does not report training time, GPU memory, or inference latency, which is critical for IIoT edge deployment.

Response: change made. Good questions, and both are now answered directly. Section 4.4 states the architecture: for each community we fit a separate small VaDE, each with its own encoder, Gaussian mixture, and scoring heads, its regime count and latent dimension scaled to the community size; nothing is shared across communities except the input window features, so the model is a stack of independent per-subsystem experts plus one global expert. The encoder is therefore not shared. Cost is reported in Appendix B and Table B1: parameter count, training wall-clock, per-window inference latency, peak GPU memory, and host memory for LatAD (global and regime-community) and the deep baselines, on one shared GPU machine plus a CPU edge proxy. LatAD-global trains in 3.2 / 16 / 3.5 s on WADI / HAI / SWaT (roughly 47 to 72 times faster than TranAD), per-window latency is 0.7 to 13 ms global and 17 to 32 ms for the full community stack, and both configurations run within about 3.5 to 3.9 GB of host memory.
