# Impact audit: does LatAD (IoT2.html) make its impact clear to its four target audiences?

Scope: read-only assessment of `poc/paper/IoT2.html` (MDPI *IoT* submission), current revision2 state.
Sections referenced: Abstract; §1 Introduction; §6 Results; §7 Drift; §8 Discussion; §9 Conclusion.

For each audience: (a) current framing + location, (b) gaps, (c) 1-3 concrete, anchored sentences to add, with placement.

---

## 1. CPS / ICS anomaly-detection researchers (USAD/TranAD/GDN/OmniAnomaly lineage)

**(a) Current framing.** This audience is the best served of the four. The abstract states the mechanism claim directly: "reconstruction-based detectors measure reachability rather than probability and miss it," backed by numbers (HAI difficult 0.845 vs. USAD/TranAD 0.44-0.50). §1 devotes two full paragraphs (the "two obstacles" paragraphs, lines 164-172) to why reconstruction fails and why the evaluation protocol is broken, citing Kim et al./Garg et al./Wu & Keogh directly. §8 "Why reconstruction fails and density wins" and "Why local density beats global density" spell out the mechanism with numbers (27% of HAI's difficult anomalies reconstruct at typical-normal level yet sit above the 90th density percentile). The reachability-vs-probability distinction is even illustrated with a synthetic-data figure (Figure A1, referenced in §8).

**(b) Gaps.** The impact *for this community specifically* — i.e., "what should a methods researcher building the next detector take away and do differently" — is implicit but never stated as a takeaway. The paper proves reconstruction has a specific, characterized blind spot (reconstructable-but-improbable joint-structure faults) and that this blind spot is where GDN, a graph-structured detector, *also* fails (§2.4: GDN collapses to 0.481 on HAI alongside USAD/TranAD) — this is a strong, underused point: it shows the failure isn't reconstruction-vs-graph-structure but reconstruction-vs-density, a broader claim than "our architecture is better." That generalization is stated once in §8 ("a graph detector failing on the same reconstructable-but-improbable faults" — this text is actually marked `rev-del`, i.e., it may have been *cut* in the current revision) but is never elevated to a stated implication for detector design in general (e.g., "any future detector, whatever its temporal or graph architecture, inherits this blind spot if it scores via reconstruction").

**(c) Suggested additions.**
1. In §8, "Why reconstruction fails and density wins" (near line 1064, after the reachability/probability crossover sentence): *"Because the blind spot is a property of the reconstruction objective, not of any one architecture, it is expected to recur in reconstruction-scored detectors with richer temporal or graph structure (e.g., transformer- or graph-based) unless they are re-scored by density; §2.4 shows this directly for the graph-structured GDN, which collapses to 0.481 on the same HAI difficult subset."* (Anchored: GDN 0.481 figure is already reported in §2.4/Table 2.)
2. In the Conclusion (§9, end of paragraph 2, after "the residual is competitive and LatAD gates it back in"): *"For the methods community, the actionable implication is a scoring-layer one: any detector — reconstruction-, transformer-, or graph-based — can be re-scored by a calibrated density in its own learned representation without discarding its architecture, and the raw-metric, difficulty-stratified protocol used here isolates whether that re-scoring is what closes the gap."* This turns the finding into a design recipe rather than only a result.
3. (Optional, abstract) The abstract could gain one clause naming the generality of the mechanism, but this is lower priority since it is already implicit via "reconstruction-based detectors" (plural, general class) rather than "USAD/TranAD."

**Verdict: impact is clear**, mechanism-wise; the missing piece is turning "we show detector X fails" into "here is what any future detector should do about it."

## 2. PHM / condition-monitoring / predictive-maintenance community (incipient fault, degradation, prognostics)

**(a) Current framing.** Present but scattered and mostly implicit. §1 gestures at this audience early: "These are the faults that matter most for condition monitoring and predictive maintenance: early warnings that stay inside familiar sensor ranges while violating the joint structure of normal operation." §9 Conclusion repeats it: "Two properties make the detector well-suited to condition monitoring and predictive maintenance: scoring against the nearest regime keeps rare-but-valid states... from paging an operator, and the regime-community density catches in-envelope, subsystem-local faults." §2.4 cites vibration-based diagnostics, structural-health monitoring, and rotorcraft fault prediction as the applied PHM line the paper "generalizes." §1's scope note explicitly separates faults from attacks and calls out "a drifting sensor, a worn actuator, or a slowly developing process fault" and cites incipient-fault literature [r-incip].

The drift-spectrum contribution (§7, C3) is the paper's single most PHM-relevant result and is framed as a detection-recovery mechanism (drift confounds detection, typing recovers it), but it is **not** explicitly framed as a degradation-characterization tool in its own right — which is exactly what PHM/condition-monitoring readers would want (drift magnitude and localization as a *diagnostic*, not just a nuisance to filter out).

**(b) Gaps.** Three specific, real results are under-sold to this audience:
- The physically-grounded drift attribution in §7.1 ("the drifting communities are exactly those built from the analyser channels... and the RO flow and pressure channels... matching the physical signature of reverse-osmosis membrane fouling and analyser electrode drift") is a genuine degradation-diagnosis result — it identifies *which subsystem is aging and how* from unsupervised telemetry alone. This is a PHM result (component-level degradation localization) but it currently reads only as a validation check on the drift-typing mechanism, not as a deliverable capability.
- Subsystem localization (Table 5, §8) is framed for "operators" (triage) but is equally a PHM capability (narrowing a developing fault to a subsystem for maintenance dispatch) — the paper does not use PHM vocabulary here (e.g., "fault localization," "root-cause narrowing") even though the numbers (68% top-3 hit rate on HAI vs. 56% random) support it.
- No explicit contrast is drawn between an abrupt attack/fault (changepoint) and a gradual degradation trajectory (drift) as two PHM-relevant target classes, even though §7.2's decomposition produces exactly that distinction mechanically.

**(c) Suggested additions.**
1. In §7.1, after the RO/electrode-drift sentence (line ~977): *"This is not merely a confound to be typed away: the same causal decomposition that recovers detection under drift also localizes which physical subsystem is degrading and at what rate, from unsupervised telemetry and without any drift or degradation labels — a capability directly usable for degradation trending and maintenance scheduling, distinct from the attack/fault detection task."* (Anchored: this is exactly what §7.1 already reports; the addition names the PHM use case.)
2. In §9 Conclusion, extend the existing PHM sentence: *"...the regime-community density catches in-envelope, subsystem-local faults that stay inside every alarm limit, and the drift-aware typing of §7 separates a genuine incipient fault from ordinary component aging in the same score, which is the two-way discrimination condition monitoring most needs."* (Anchored: §7.2's changepoint-vs-drift typing is exactly this discrimination.)
3. In the Discussion "Subsystem localization" paragraph (line 1152), add one clause connecting to PHM vocabulary: *"...a shortlist directly usable for fault localization and maintenance dispatch, the same task PHM systems perform when narrowing a developing fault to a component (§2.4)."*

**Verdict: impact is implicit, not clear.** The paper has PHM-grade results (degradation attribution to a physical mechanism, subsystem localization) but frames them as internal validation of the detection mechanism rather than as PHM deliverables. This is the community with the largest gap between what the paper delivers and what it explicitly claims.

## 3. IIoT telemetry community (edge/gateway/SCADA, streaming multivariate sensor data, on-device inference)

**(a) Current framing.** This is the journal's own audience (MDPI *IoT*) and the paper works hardest here textually, but much of that work is recent surface-level rewriting (visible via the `rev-highlight`/`rev2-highlight` spans) rather than new substance. The abstract opens with "The Industrial Internet of Things streams multivariate telemetry from physical processes" — an explicit IIoT framing sentence. §1 has a full paragraph on the IIoT deployment picture (field sensors → PLCs → edge gateway/SCADA historian, Figure 1) and states "unsupervised anomaly detection is the operative safeguard for IIoT reliability and security." §2.1 is a dedicated related-work subsection, "Anomaly detection in the Industrial IoT and edge intelligence," covering edge inference cost tradeoffs and fleet heterogeneity. §8's "Implications for IIoT deployment" paragraph and Table B1/Appendix B (training/inference cost on a CPU edge proxy, GPU latency, host memory) are concrete, measured deliverables: train-normal-only commissioning, per-window online scoring, and specific edge-relevant numbers (0.6-14.6 ms/window CPU-edge latency, 3.5-3.9 GB host memory, 47-72x faster training than TranAD).

**(b) Gaps.**
- The cost table (Appendix B) is real and IIoT-relevant, but the Discussion paragraph that should sell it ("Implications for IIoT deployment," line 1142) spends most of its words re-describing the architecture diagram (Figure 1) rather than stating what the *numbers* mean for a deployer: e.g., it never says explicitly "this places LatAD-global within X ms real-time budget for Y sampling rate," leaving the reader to infer feasibility from raw latency numbers alone.
- The regime-community (full, factorized) variant — the one that actually wins on the difficult subset — is *not* the one benchmarked for edge cost; Table B1 measures "LatAD (global density)" only. The paper flags this itself ("the regime-community realization instantiates one density model per community, so its inference cost grows with the number of subsystems... where that footprint matters, adaptive-inference optimizations are a natural fit") but this is a caveat, not a resolved cost claim, and an IIoT reader will want to know the actual per-community-model inference cost, not just a pointer to future early-exit work.
- The abstract does not mention edge/on-device deployability at all — it is method- and results-focused, with the IIoT framing limited to the opening sentence and the word "Industrial-IoT" in the title/keywords. A reader skimming only the abstract (the majority of readers) will not learn that the paper measures edge feasibility.

**(c) Suggested additions.**
1. In the abstract, one added clause near the end (before "Code and checkpoints are released"): *"Commissioning uses train-normal telemetry only and per-window scoring runs within gateway-class latency and memory budgets (Appendix B), supporting deployment at the edge/SCADA tier."* (Anchored: Appendix B's measured CPU-edge latency and host-memory figures.)
2. In §8 "Implications for IIoT deployment" (after the Figure 1 sentence, line 1150): *"Concretely, the CPU-edge-proxy latency of 0.6-25 ms per window (Table B1) is well inside the multi-second-to-minute windowing cadence typical of SCADA historian aggregation, so the reported model runs on gateway-class hardware without requiring GPU inference at the edge."* (Anchored: the 0.6-14.6/14.5-25 ms CPU-edge figures already reported in Appendix B; this sentence translates them into a deployability claim, an inference the paper supports but does not state.)
3. In Appendix B or the same Discussion paragraph, add the actual regime-community (not just global) edge cost, or, if not measured, an explicit statement bounding it: *"The regime-community configuration's inference cost scales with community count S (Table B1 reports the global-density baseline; per-community costs are additive and bounded by S times the global-model figures, since communities score independently)."* This closes the "we didn't measure the deployed variant" gap honestly rather than leaving it as a pure caveat.

**Verdict: impact is present but under-stated at the point readers look first (abstract).** The paper has done the actual IIoT-relevant measurement work (Appendix B); it needs to surface it earlier and translate raw ms/GB numbers into a deployability statement.

## 4. Practitioners / operators (deploy, act on alarms, commission from normal-only data, subsystem triage, false-alarm budgets)

**(a) Current framing.** Partially explicit, concentrated in §8. Three operator-relevant results exist and are reported with numbers: (i) train-normal-only commissioning (stated repeatedly, e.g., Figure 1 caption, §8's IIoT-deployment paragraph); (ii) subsystem triage via Table 5 (68% top-3 hit rate on HAI vs. 56% random, explicitly called "a triage shortlist rather than a diagnosis"); (iii) false-alarm-budget numbers appear in a few places — the 5% false-alarm-budget true-positive-rate figures in the "Benchmark coverage" paragraph (0.789 → 0.916 after excluding an under-covered regime), and the drift-typing false-alarm reduction in Table 4 (SWaT normal false-alarm rate 79% → 6.5% after changepoint typing).

**(b) Gaps.**
- These three practitioner-relevant results are never gathered into one place or explicitly named as "what an operator gets." Each appears inside a Discussion subsection whose heading is about something else (drift mechanism, benchmark coverage, subsystem localization) — an operator-focused reader has to assemble the practitioner story from three separate paragraphs.
- The false-alarm-budget framing is the single most operator-relevant number in the paper (a 79%→6.5% false-alarm rate is dramatic and directly answers "will this alarm-fatigue my team?") but it sits inside Table 4 of the drift section, framed as validation of the typing mechanism, not flagged anywhere as "this is what changes for an operator running SWaT-like plants."
- The Limitations paragraph (§8, "no point adjustment... reported numbers are lower than point-adjusted leaderboard figures") is honest and correctly placed, but there is no practitioner-facing translation of what an F1 of, e.g., 0.36-0.42 on the difficult subset *means operationally* (e.g., expected false-alarm rate at a given recall, or how the triage shortlist changes an operator's workflow versus a plant-wide alarm). A practitioner reading only Table 2's F1 column with no operational translation may under- or over-estimate deployability.
- The subsystem-localization caveats (i)/(ii) in §8 are honest and good practice, but the paper never states the flip side positively: *how much operator effort the shortlist saves* relative to no localization at all (e.g., "instead of inspecting all 59 channels/N subsystems, an operator inspects 3 communities averaging 5.6 channels each").

**(c) Suggested additions.**
1. In §9 Conclusion or as a new short paragraph at the end of §8 (before "Limitations"), gather the three results into one explicit practitioner statement: *"For an operator, three properties matter jointly: a site is commissioned from its own normal telemetry with no labeled attacks (Figure 1); a flagged window comes with a three-community shortlist that contains the true subsystem in 68% of HAI attacks against 56% by chance (Table 5), reducing triage from all 59 channels to an average of 5.6; and under drift, the same scoring pipeline cuts the false-alarm rate from 79% to 6.5% of normal windows on SWaT (Table 4) without added labels."* (Anchored: all three numbers are already reported; this sentence is pure aggregation, not new claims.)
2. In the Limitations paragraph (§8, line 1191), add one sentence translating F1 to an operational quantity: *"At the reported best-raw-F1 operating point, the difficult-subset precision/recall tradeoff (F1 0.36-0.42 on HAI/WADI) means an operator sees more false alarms per true alarm on the difficult subset than on the easy majority; the subsystem shortlist (Table 5) is the mitigation this paper offers for that tradeoff, not a claim of a low false-alarm difficult-subset operating point."* This is honest (does not inflate) and directly useful to a deployer deciding whether to trust difficult-subset alarms.
3. (Smaller, optional) In the abstract or Figure 1 caption, state the false-alarm reduction number once, since it is the single most persuasive practitioner-facing statistic in the paper and currently appears only in a table deep in §7.

**Verdict: impact is present in the results but not assembled or stated for this audience.** This is the community whose relevant numbers are most scattered; a single consolidating paragraph would fix most of the gap without adding any new claims.

## Summary (for the calling report)

| Audience | Impact clear? | Highest-value single addition |
|---|---|---|
| 1. CPS/ICS methods researchers | Yes, mechanism-level | Generalize the reconstruction-blind-spot finding beyond USAD/TranAD/GDN into an explicit design implication for future detectors (§8 or §9). |
| 2. PHM / condition monitoring | Implicit, real gap | Reframe §7.1's physically-grounded drift attribution (RO fouling / electrode drift) as a degradation-localization *deliverable*, not just validation of the typing mechanism. |
| 3. IIoT telemetry | Present but buried until Appendix B | Pull the edge-cost numbers (CPU-edge ms/window, host RAM) into the abstract or the front of §8's deployment paragraph as an explicit deployability statement. |
| 4. Practitioners/operators | Scattered across 3 sections | Add one consolidating paragraph (end of §8) that states commissioning, triage shortlist, and false-alarm reduction together as "what an operator gets," using only numbers already in the paper. |

**Overall single highest-value addition across all four audiences**: the practitioner-consolidation paragraph (Audience 4, item 1) — it requires no new analysis, fabricates nothing, uses only numbers already computed (Table 4, Table 5, Figure 1's commissioning claim), and is the paper's most concrete, decision-relevant IIoT/PHM claim once assembled: normal-only commissioning + subsystem triage (68% vs 56% chance) + a measured false-alarm cut (79%→6.5% under drift). Currently no single sentence in the manuscript states this three-part case in one place, even though every part of it is already proven.

All suggested sentences above are anchored strictly in numbers/claims already present in the manuscript (Table 2-5, Appendix A/B/C, §6-§8); none introduces a new experimental claim.
