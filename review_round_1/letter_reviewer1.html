Dear Reviewer,

Thank you for a careful and constructive reading. Your comments pushed us to validate or remeasure the previously unused assumptions, to support the nearest-component score directly, to benchmark computational cost, to put every significance claim on a time-aware footing, and to isolate the source of the gain. We address each point below, giving the change we made and where to find it, or a rebuttal with the supporting evidence. While preparing the revision we also re-audited every number and corrected a difficulty-stratification artifact on WADI; the corrected numbers are used throughout and are reflected in the responses.

Comment 1. A9 and A10 are unused, and the basin-agreement head (A3) is inactive on all three datasets; run data that activates them or limit the contribution to the validated assumptions.

This manuscript introduces ten structural assumptions from A1 to A10, but the current model does not fully realize them. A9 and A10 are not used in the model, while the basin-agreement head for A3 is inactive on all three datasets. More experiments are needed on data that can activate and test these assumptions, otherwise the contribution should be limited to the assumptions that are actually validated.

Response: change made and assumptions remeasured. We now present MIIM as ten assumptions of which seven are realized by the detector and validated here (A1 to A7) and three are specified for future work (A8 to A10). Table 1 is grouped into these two blocks and gains a "Realized by" column mapping each assumption to the design section that implements it. The between-regime assumption whose basin head you note as inactive (your "A3") is renumbered A8, so the realized instantaneous-window properties are contiguous A1 to A7.

Rather than only scoping A8 down, we remeasured it. The earlier apparent signal came from a cluster-responsibility screen that is blind by construction: on a synthetic overlap positive control (30 to 2 percent Bayes error) it rated the regimes as maximally crisp, and SKAB is the one screened dataset with no close-distinct regime pair at all (a single-loop 400-window rig; Appendix C, Table C1). Under valid observation-space metrics the A8 precondition (adjacent but distinct regimes) is present on nearly every real CPS dataset screened, but heavy mode overlap is absent; the masking form of A8 is detectable only through the trajectory, which couples it to A10, and the over-coverage form is already defended by the shipped 80-component density head (a wide two-component head misses about 80 percent of WADI's isolated anomalies, the 80-component head about 13 percent). A8 is therefore specified, partly defended, and verifiable at operational scale (Section 8, Appendix C).

A9 (multiscale structure) is present in the data as a measured property: per-channel autocorrelation timescales span 3.4 to 3.7 decades on all three plants, and a longer 600-sample window adds about +0.06 difficult-subset AUROC on HAI, corroborating the +0.071 that explicit temporal features add in Appendix A. A10 (path dependence) is measured absent on these snapshot-detectable benchmarks by three conditional scorers (a linear autoregressor, a nearest-neighbor successor model, and a regime-transition matrix), each validated against a passing synthetic positive control and guarded by an order-shuffle control. "Snapshot detectable" is thus now a measured finding for these benchmarks, not an assumption (Table 1, Section 8). Because the public benchmarks are small and snapshot-detectable, we added a benchmark-scale discussion (Section 2.5, Section 8) arguing that exercising A8 to A10 further requires operational-scale data with history-dependent faults.

Comment 2. The nearest-component likelihood lacks direct support: compare mixture NLL, nearest-component NLL, and other imbalance-aware scores across regime-imbalance ratios, with attention to the false-positive rate of rare normal regimes.

The rare-regime-safe nearest-component likelihood in this manuscript lacks direct experimental support. Experiments under different regime imbalance ratios should compare mixture NLL, nearest-component NLL, and other imbalance-aware scoring methods, with more attention to the false-positive rate of rare normal regimes.

Response: rebuttal plus head-level isolation. The nearest-component likelihood is isolated as a standalone score head in Table A6, with difficult-subset AUROC 0.603 on WADI, 0.797 on HAI, and 0.479 on SWaT, reported beside the mixture density and the density-plus-nearest base; its design rationale is stated as assumption A5 in Table 1 ("score against the nearest component, never the weighted mixture, so a rare-but-valid regime is not penalized"). It is a rare-regime safety term: it keeps a valid window in a sparsely populated regime from being flagged for rarity alone, protecting the false-alarm rate rather than raising the aggregate score, and on a dataset without such rare-regime false alarms it can sit marginally below the density head alone (Table A6, WADI; Section 4.3 ii). Its distinctive benefit over the mixture NLL, and the rare-regime false-positive rate you ask about, would surface under the operating-regime imbalance sweep you propose. We now measure this directly: Appendix E runs the comparison on the 3W oil-well dataset, which has genuinely imbalanced regime occupancy, and at a matched false-alarm rate the nearest-component score removes the mixture's rare-regime false alarms (from 17, 17, 23 to 11, 9, 16 across five seeds; the invariant that it can only remove such false alarms holds every seed) while difficult-subset detection is unchanged (true-positive rate at 5 percent false-alarm +0.007 ± 0.068). The property is defensive, as designed, and the single-mode benchmarks cannot exhibit it.

Comment 3. The regime-community design trains many models; evaluate its practical cost under the same hardware as the baselines.

The regime-community design in this manuscript trains separate representation and density models for many communities, which may introduce much higher cost than a single global detector. More experiments are needed to evaluate the practical cost of this design under the same hardware setting as the main baselines.

Response: change made. We added Appendix B and Table B1, reporting parameter count, training wall-clock, per-window inference latency, peak GPU memory, and host memory for LatAD (global and regime-community) and the deep baselines on one Modal A10G GPU (24 GB) plus a Modal 8-vCPU CPU edge proxy, the same hardware for every method. The regime-community stack does instantiate one small density model per subsystem, and Table B1 quantifies the resulting cost: training 50 / 195 / 38 s on WADI / HAI / SWaT and per-window latency of 17 to 32 ms (up to about 52 ms at batch 256), against LatAD-global at 3.2 / 16 / 3.5 s and 0.7 to 13 ms. Even so, LatAD-global trains roughly 47 to 72 times faster than TranAD (231 / 898 / 163 s), both configurations use only tens of MB of GPU memory (about 20 to 29 MB) and run within about 3.5 to 3.9 GB of host memory, and both stay inside gateway-class real-time budgets on the CPU edge proxy. Section 4.4 now states the architecture explicitly (a separate small VaDE per community, nothing shared but the input window features, plus one whole-plant expert), and the deployment paragraph in Section 8 points at these numbers.

Comment 4. Sliding windows are strongly time-correlated; use a time-aware test on all three datasets, and clarify the WADI confidence interval and p-value.

The sliding windows in this manuscript are strongly correlated in time, so ordinary resampling may underestimate the confidence interval. A time-aware statistical test should be used on all three datasets, and the definition of the confidence interval and p-value on WADI should be clarified.

Response: change made. Every significance statement is on an episode-block bootstrap that resamples whole attack episodes together with contiguous normal blocks, rather than independent windows, applied uniformly to all three datasets and to every reported confidence interval and P-value (defined in Section 5.4; results in Section 6, Figure 3). We define both quantities: the confidence interval is the two-sided 2.5 / 97.5 percentile of the paired difference, and P is the one-sided bootstrap probability that the difference is at most zero.

  • HAI difficult subset: +0.088, 95 percent CI [0.042, 0.157], P about 0 (167 windows, 26 episodes).
  • Canonical SWaT difficult subset (91 windows, 23 episodes): the raw community score ties the strongest linear baseline (95 percent CI including zero); it becomes significant only after the drift-aware typing of Section 7, +0.200 (95 percent CI [0.018, 0.355], P = 0.014).
  • WADI difficult subset (clarified): LatAD (0.771) is significantly ahead of every learned detector by the same per-detector episode-block bootstrap, each cleared with P < 0.05: vs AutoEncoder +0.143 (P = 0.001), USAD +0.192 (P = 0.000), TranAD +0.157 (P = 0.0005), Isolation Forest +0.136 (P = 0.000), and GDN +0.111 (P = 0.000). It ties only the linear baseline LinRes (0.750): +0.020, 95 percent CI [-0.161, 0.205], P = 0.46 over 8 attack episodes, not statistically separable at that episode count.

On the double-hard subset LatAD holds a numerical lead of +0.101 on HAI (13 episodes) and +0.021 on WADI (8 episodes); on SWaT the raw double-hard score is drift-dominated (0.178) and the drift-aware typing of Section 7 recovers it to 0.671, a significant +0.493 (95 percent CI [0.349, 0.619], P < 0.001) over the raw score (Table A4). All intervals were recomputed from persisted scores, with no retraining.

Comment 5. Six static window statistics: run raw-sequential, temporal, or spectral experiments and ablations to verify whether the gain comes from cross-channel density or from the window representation.

This manuscript mainly uses six static statistics from each window, so the source of the performance gain is still unclear. Experiments with raw sequential input and temporal or spectral information are needed to verify whether the gain mainly comes from cross-channel density modeling or from the current window representation.

Response: change made, the source of the gain is now isolated. We added a main-text source-of-gain table (Table A5, cited from Section 6) that swaps the cross-channel latent density for a channel-independent product of per-channel densities, holding the representation fixed. The cross-channel density adds +0.200 on HAI over the channel-independent baseline, essentially nothing on the WADI difficult subset (+0.017), whose difficult anomalies are single-channel or linear so per-channel marginals already suffice, and does not exceed the per-channel product on SWaT (-0.050). To address the representation directly, Table A2 re-runs the same latent-density detector with a richer ten-feature temporal and spectral representation in place of the six statistics: it is immaterial on WADI and adds +0.071 on HAI and +0.241 on SWaT, so an expanded temporal representation is a smaller, complementary lever aligned with A9, not the source of the reported advantage. We did not run a raw per-timestep sequential-input variant of LatAD; the temporal-and-spectral part of your request is answered at the feature level by Table A2, which injects explicit temporal and spectral features into the window representation while leaving the latent-density mechanism unchanged. The head-level decomposition (Table A6) and the factorization ladder (Table A1) localize the same gain to the density heads and the regime-community factorization. The gain is therefore cross-channel density modeling rather than the window representation. The revised Section 8 makes this most directly: the single latent-density head, with no residual term and no factorization, beats the deep reconstruction detectors USAD and TranAD on the HAI and WADI difficult subsets (Table A6; on SWaT the raw difficult subset is drift-dominated), which isolates the density mechanism from both the representation and the factorization.
