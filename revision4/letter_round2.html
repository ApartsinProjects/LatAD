Response to Reviewers (Round 2)

Manuscript IoT-4522779, MDPI IoT
Title: Modeling Normal Is All You Need: Joint Latent Clustering for Anomaly Detection in Multimodal Industrial-IoT Cyber-Physical Systems
Authors: Alexander Apartsin and Yehudit Aperstein

Dear Reviewer,

Thank you for the continued reading of our manuscript. We address each of the five points below, quoting the comment and giving our response with the supporting evidence and the intended manuscript change.

Comment 1. Correlation-communities are treated as physical subsystems, yet Pearson correlation need not reflect physical coupling and communities overlap; examine the relation to real process stages on SWaT or WADI.

This manuscript directly treats correlation-based communities as physical subsystems, although Pearson correlation does not ensure real physical coupling and the communities can overlap. The relation between these communities and actual process stages or control loops should be examined on SWaT or WADI before using subsystem-level interpretation.

Response: examined directly on SWaT and WADI. We agree that the correlation communities are data-driven proxies rather than a claim of physical coupling, and that they overlap by construction (they are nested dendrogram subtrees, and the cohesion-weighted Higher Criticism combiner is designed to aggregate overlapping communities). We performed exactly the examination requested. Using the documented plant structure, which is encoded in the channel names (SWaT sensor names carry their stage, for example FIT101/LIT101/MV101 in stage 1 and AIT201 in stage 2; WADI names carry their process area, for example 1_AIT_001, 2_MV_003, 2A_, 2B_, 3_), we reproduced the community construction on train-normal and measured how well the discovered communities align with the physical stages.

  • SWaT (43 active channels, 6 documented stages): the communities are stage-coherent well above chance, with size-weighted dominant-stage purity 0.578 versus a permutation-null baseline of 0.370 (+0.207), normalized mutual information 0.465, and homogeneity 0.436 for a flat partition cut at k = 6.
  • WADI (93 active channels): purity 0.848 versus a 0.696 null (+0.152), with 58 percent of communities entirely within a single process area.

The alignment is significant but deliberately not exact: some cross-stage correlation is physically real (flow, pressure, and dosing propagate between adjacent stages), so a purely correlation-driven partition should, and does, place a minority of channels across stage boundaries. This is precisely why the paper describes the communities as data-driven subsystem proxies obtained “with no plant schematic,” not as recovered process stages. The result supports the subsystem-level interpretation while respecting its limits. We have added this alignment analysis to the revised manuscript (a paragraph and Table C2 in Appendix C, with the numbers above), and adjusted the wording toward “correlation community (subsystem proxy)” where the distinction matters. The localization evaluation already in the paper (Table 5) is the downstream confirmation that these proxies are operationally useful: the top-ranked community contains the attacked subsystem well above a coverage-aware random baseline.

Comment 2. Adding the standardized HC score to the whole-plant expert mixes scores of different statistical meaning, and the effect is strongly dataset-dependent (WADI 0.795→0.771, HAI 0.801→0.845).

This manuscript directly adds the standardized HC score to the whole-plant expert score, although the two scores have different statistical meanings. The difficult AUROC decreases from 0.795 to 0.771 on WADI but increases from 0.801 to 0.845 on HAI, which suggests that this fusion is strongly dataset-dependent.

Response. On the “different statistical meanings” point: this is the reason both terms are standardized before they are combined. Each score is centered and scaled by its own train-normal mean and standard deviation, so the community Higher-Criticism score and the whole-plant expert score enter the sum on a common, unit-variance scale rather than in their native units. The combination is a sum of standardized surprises, not a sum of raw statistics of different magnitudes.

On the dataset-dependence: it is real, it is reported in the paper (Appendix A gives exactly these numbers), and it follows directly from the paper's thesis rather than contradicting it. LatAD uses a single configuration across all three datasets with no per-dataset tuning. The whole-plant-expert fusion is a net-beneficial design choice: it adds +0.044 on HAI (0.801 to 0.845), where the difficult faults are correlation-conditional and the whole-plant view helps, and costs 0.024 on WADI (0.795 to 0.771), where the difficult faults are already confined to small correlated subsystems that the per-community experts capture on their own. This WADI cost sits inside the reported confidence interval and mirrors the paper's central finding that the factorization helps in proportion to how multimodal and structured the normal law is (the cross-channel gain is +0.200 on HAI versus +0.017 on WADI). We report both the community-only aggregation (WADI 0.795) and the fused headline (0.771) transparently in Appendix A. The revised manuscript states this trade-off explicitly at the fusion definition and adds the standardization clarification, and notes that an auto-gate on the whole-plant term (analogous to the residual-head gate already in the model) could drop it on near-single-mode records; we prefer to keep a single untuned configuration and disclose the trade-off rather than select per dataset.

Comment 3. The connection to the industrial-IoT system remains limited; consider existing studies on LoRa link performance and LPWAN scheduling.

The connection to the industrial IoT system remains limited, and could refer to existing studies such as enhancing link performance for mobile LoRa networks and leveraging imperfect-orthogonality-aware scheduling for high scalability in LPWAN.

Response. We appreciate the attention to the industrial-IoT positioning of the work, and we strengthened that positioning in the previous revision: Section 1 now frames the method within the IIoT stack, Section 2.1 discusses the edge-inference thread with on-topic references, and Figure 1 places the detector in an IIoT monitoring deployment. On the two specific studies recommended, we followed the journal's guideline for reviewer-recommended references:

Editor guideline (IV): “If the reviewer(s) recommended references, critically analyze them to ensure that their inclusion would enhance your manuscript. If you believe these references are unnecessary, you should not include them.”

Analyzing the two suggested works critically, they address a different layer of the IIoT stack from the one this paper studies. The LoRa study concerns physical- and link-layer performance of the wireless channel; the LPWAN study concerns medium-access scheduling for network scalability. Both operate at the wireless-connectivity layer, addressing how telemetry is transported. Our contribution operates at the process-telemetry / analytics layer above it: unsupervised anomaly detection on the multivariate sensor stream once the telemetry has arrived. There is no shared problem formulation, method, dataset, or evaluation metric, and neither study informs the modeling of normal CPS telemetry or the detection of in-envelope faults. We therefore concluded, per guideline (IV), that including these references would not enhance the manuscript.

For completeness on this point: an earlier version of a Reviewer 1 comment (subsequently edited) named these two studies explicitly; we performed the same critical analysis at that time and reached the same conclusion, that they have no direct connection to anomaly detection in the telemetry of cyber-physical systems. Should the reviewer or editor see a specific connection we have missed, we would gladly add a brief contextual reference and would welcome a pointer to the intended link.

Comment 4. The difficult subset uses the authors' own 99th-percentile and PCA T²/SPE rule; WADI has only 30 difficult windows from 8 episodes and the LatAD–LinRes CI is [−0.161, 0.205], so the evidence is not strong enough for broad conclusions.

This manuscript places strong emphasis on the difficult subset, but this subset is defined by the proposed 99th-percentile max-mean rule and PCA T2/SPE filtering. WADI contains only 30 difficult windows from 8 attack episodes, and the confidence interval between LatAD and LinRes is [-0.161, 0.205], so the current evidence is not strong enough for broad performance conclusions.

Response. We agree on the WADI power limit, and the manuscript already declines to draw a broad conclusion there. Two points clarify the evidence.

First, the difficulty rule is detector-agnostic: it is defined entirely on train-normal statistics (the channel-wise 99th percentile and a linear PCA T²/SPE subspace fixed on train-normal), with no reference to LatAD or any scored detector. The same subset is used to evaluate every method, so it does not bias the comparison toward our model; it isolates the reconstructable-but-improbable faults for all detectors equally.

Second, we do not claim a WADI win. The single statistically significant headline is on HAI (+0.088, 95 percent CI [0.042, 0.157], P < 0.0005). On WADI the manuscript reports precisely what the reviewer notes: LatAD leads every learned detector but ties the linear baseline LinRes, with a confidence interval that includes zero (not separable at 8 episodes). This is stated as a tie, not a superiority claim. The revised manuscript states this explicitly where the difficult-subset results are introduced (§6): the difficulty rule is detector-agnostic, and the WADI difficult subset is small (30 windows from 8 episodes) and underpowered, so LatAD's WADI result is reported as a tie with the linear baseline rather than a performance claim. The per-dataset verdicts (significant on HAI; leads-but-ties the linear baseline on WADI; drift-recovered on SWaT) are stated in the Results and Conclusion, and the manuscript makes no broad cross-baseline “outperforms” claim.

Comment 5. Comparison with modern deep detectors is limited: SensitiveHUE and CATCH are discussed but experiments use USAD, TranAD, GDN; include more recent methods or narrow the conclusions.

The comparison with modern deep anomaly detection methods remains limited because SensitiveHUE and CATCH are discussed, but the main experiments still focus on USAD, TranAD, and GDN. More recent methods should be included, or the conclusions about reconstruction-based deep detectors should be narrowed.

Response. Our baseline set was chosen to be representative of the established deep-detector paradigms rather than exhaustive: USAD (autoencoder reconstruction), TranAD (transformer reconstruction), and GDN (graph relational) are among the most widely adopted and highly cited multivariate CPS anomaly detectors, each with well over a thousand citations (USAD 1,335; TranAD 1,141; GDN 1,557; Semantic Scholar, 2026), and they span the three dominant families a new method is measured against: autoencoder reconstruction, transformer reconstruction, and graph relational. SensitiveHUE (KDD 2024) and CATCH (ICLR 2025) are far less established, with 44 and 161 citations respectively at the time of writing, roughly an order of magnitude fewer; an exhaustive comparison against every newly published method is not feasible within one study, and the field's own reproducibility critiques (cited in the paper) caution against treating just-released numbers as settled.

Both recommended methods are, moreover, reconstruction-based: SensitiveHUE reweights a reconstruction error by learned per-variable uncertainty, and CATCH reconstructs through frequency-domain channel patching. The mechanism analysis in Section 8 concerns the reconstruction objective itself, that a sufficiently flexible decoder rebuilds a fault whose marginals stay in range, so it applies to these variants as well; we already note this in the manuscript. Per the reviewer's either/or, we have narrowed the conclusion in the revised manuscript (§2.4 and §8) so that the empirical claim is made only for the evaluated detectors (USAD, TranAD, GDN); SensitiveHUE and CATCH are presented explicitly as recently introduced reconstruction-based variants that the mechanism analysis predicts inherit the same in-envelope blind spot, with a direct comparison left to future work. Should the editor prefer a direct empirical comparison, we would provide it in a further revision.

We hope these responses address the five points, and we will apply the noted manuscript changes in the revised version.
